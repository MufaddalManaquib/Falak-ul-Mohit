Same sky, different centre

How this Earth-centred model compares with the heliocentric one, and why it breaks no law of physics or mathematics.

Motion is relative. Every observation is a direction, a time or a delay, and those do not depend on which body is called the centre. This model changes the point of view, never the physics.

Gravity

The pull needed to swing the Sun around a still Earth once a year. Earth’s own gravity at that distance is 333,000 times too weak, so in the Earth-fixed frame the pull appears as a frame force, the same kind that presses you into your seat when a car speeds up. With it, every motion is exact; without it, Newton’s laws would fail.

1.75 arcseconds

Light

The Sun’s real bending of starlight at its edge, measured since 1919. Flexing spheres leave light exactly as it is. Round spheres only draw it curved on a map of the same space. Spheres that bent light physically would need about ten million suns of mass, or would split colours like glass; observations rule out both.

27.5 AU

The turning sky

If Earth does not spin, the sky turns once a day, and beyond 27.5 AU that turning outruns light. These are coordinate speeds: nothing outruns light locally and no signal is sent, so relativity allows it. It does mean nothing out there can sit still in the Earth-fixed frame.

Inertial frames

What physics can decide

Physics cannot name the centre. It can name the frames in which gyroscopes stay fixed: those that do not rotate against the distant galaxies. The Earth-fixed description reproduces them with frame terms, or reads them, after Mach, as frames carried round by the turning sky.

What this does and does not prove

Relative positions do not depend on which body is called the centre, so a correct Earth-centred model must agree with the heliocentric one. That is what these columns test: that holding Earth still, nesting the spheres and drawing everything on a logarithmic map loses nothing a telescope can measure. Both columns use the same planetary theory, so neither is an independent observation. The independent test is the comparison with NASA JPL Horizons, which is fitted to radar ranging, spacecraft tracking and laser ranging to the Moon. The paper sets out the mathematics in full.

Geometry

Blendspheres

Round spheres keep perfect spheres; depth means rank, so light paths curve on this map. Flexing spheres keep true distances and straight light; each sphere flexes just enough to hold its planet, and the spheres never touch. From Earth both look identical, and with the Sun as centre the flexing view shows every orbit as its true ellipse.

Frame

The Muhit turns once per sidereal day (23 h 56 m) and carries every sphere with it.
