What gravity and light do in each picture, and why nothing you can measure tells them apart.

One universe, three descriptions

Every telescope measurement is a direction, a time, a brightness, a colour or a delay. These are events, and physics predicts them the same way whichever point you call the centre. This site shows the same events in three ways:

  • Heliocentric (strictly, the solar system's centre of mass): the frame physicists use, because in it the laws take their simplest form.
  • Flexing spheres: Earth held still and true distances kept. Each sphere is a surface drawn through ordinary space that bends just enough to carry its planet. Light and orbits are untouched.
  • Round spheres: Earth held still, with each sphere a perfect sphere. To make that work, depth is turned into rank. This is a curved map of the same space, like a Mercator map of the globe.

Gravity

In the heliocentric frame, gravity is one inverse-square pull from the Sun, plus small pulls between planets. Earth's speed of 29.8 km/s around a 1 AU orbit needs an acceleration of 5.9 mm/s² toward the Sun, and the Sun's gravity supplies exactly that.

Now hold Earth still. The Sun must circle Earth once a year, so something must accelerate the Sun by 5.9 mm/s². Earth's own gravity at 1 AU is only 0.000018 mm/s², about 333,000 times too weak. That factor is exactly the Sun's mass divided by Earth's. If the only forces allowed were the planets' real gravity, the Earth-centred picture would break Newton's laws.

It doesn't have to break them, because Earth is not an inertial frame. A frame that moves with Earth is accelerating, and every accelerating frame comes with fictitious forces, the same kind that pushes you back in your seat when a car speeds up.

  • In the Earth-centred frame, every body feels an extra uniform 5.9 mm/s² aimed away from the Sun. This is Earth's own acceleration, seen from Earth, and it carries the Sun around its yearly circle.
  • If Earth's daily turn is also frozen, as in the "Earth fixed" frame here, centrifugal and Coriolis terms appear. These are what swing Foucault's pendulum and twist hurricanes.

With these terms included, the Earth-centred description gives exactly the same motions and is fully consistent. Einstein's general relativity goes further: any coordinates are allowed, and the frame terms simply become part of how gravity is written in those coordinates. Earth is in free fall, so locally it is as good a laboratory as any.

Light

In every description light moves at 299,792 km/s locally. Near the Sun it is bent by 1.75″ and delayed slightly. Both effects were predicted by relativity and are now measured: the bending to about 0.01 %, and the delay by the Cassini spacecraft to about 0.002 %. Heliocentric and flexing spheres agree on all of this, because their space is the same ordinary space.

In the round-spheres map, light paths are drawn curved, sometimes by tens of degrees. That curving belongs to the map, not to space. The quantity that measures real curvature, the Riemann tensor, is unchanged. No photon feels anything new, so every colour and every radar pulse arrives exactly as before. This is the "curved map of space" option in the light drawer.

The spheres could not be physical things that bend light. A real lens made of mass would need thousands of Suns' worth of matter, which would wreck every orbit. A real crystal would split colours and slow radar by frequency. Measurements rule out both, which is why the model treats the spheres as geometry, not matter.

Where the Earth-fixed sky becomes strange

If Earth does not spin, the whole sky must turn once a day. At 27.5 AU, just inside Neptune's orbit, that daily circuit already moves at the speed of light. Proxima Centauri would race around at nearly 10,000 times light speed, and distant galaxies far faster.

Relativity allows this because these are coordinate speeds. Nothing outruns light locally, and no signal is sent. It does mean that beyond 27.5 AU no real object can sit still in that frame. The rotating-Earth description and the turning-sky description cover the same events, but only one of them keeps physics simple out there.

What physics can and cannot decide

Physics cannot pick an absolute centre. Motion is relative, and every frame here predicts the same sky. What physics can pick out are the inertial frames. In those frames gyroscopes don't drift, pendulums don't precess, and no fictitious forces are needed. They are the frames that do not rotate against the distant galaxies, and the solar system's centre of mass moves almost exactly inertially. That is why astronomers use the heliocentric picture: it is the simplest frame to calculate in.

This model chooses differently. It keeps Earth at the centre and the nine spheres as its organising geometry. The flexing view does this without touching space, light or orbits. The round view does it with a curved map. Neither adds a force or a particle, and neither breaks a measured law. What they give up is simplicity, not correctness. Al-Falak al-Muhit lies beyond the particle horizon, from where no signal could ever have reached us, so no observation can confirm it or rule it out.
